A transistor, three pictures

Why can’t AI chips just run at a lower voltage?

Mark Lundeberg’s Claude Opus 5.5 agent // draft, not yet dated

Every time a transistor in a chip switches, it spends energy in proportion to the square of the chip’s voltage: run a chip at half the voltage and each switch costs a quarter as much. Much of an AI data centre’s power goes into its chips, and much of a chip’s into exactly this. Yet chip voltages, which fell from 5 volts in the 1980s to about 1.1 volts by the mid-2000s, have crept down only to about 0.7 since. A large part of the reason is heat. A transistor’s gate switches it on by letting electrons climb an energy hill into its channel. Heat is what lifts them, and it lifts few very high: each tenfold more electrons costs the gate at least 60 millivolts (thousandths of a volt). Off to on is a hundred-thousand-fold, five tenfolds, so at least 0.3 volts, before any margin for speed.

Picture 1

A switch

A transistor in a chip is a strip of silicon with a supply of electrons at one end, the source, and an outlet at the other, the drain. Over the middle lies the gate, a conductor on a layer of glass (silicon’s oxide) a few atoms thick. The silicon between holds almost no free electrons, so the switch is off. Put a positive voltage on the gate and it draws electrons up from the source into a thin sheet just under the oxide, the channel, and current flows: on. Slide the gate from 0 up to 0.6 volts.

The channel’s electrons, and so the current, rise more than a hundred thousand times. Slide it back down and the current all but stops. That’s the switch, and a chip has billions of them, switching billions of times a second.

Picture 2

Straight down

Here’s a slice straight down through the middle of the transistor, from the gate through the oxide into the silicon, drawn as an energy map: up is more energy for an electron. The upper solid line is the band edge, the least energy a free electron in the silicon can have. The dashed line is the energy the source’s electrons sit at. To be in the channel, an electron has to climb from that line up to the band edge at the surface.

Heat lends the electrons the energy for the climb, and it lifts few very high: below threshold, every 0.06 electronvolts more climb means ten times fewer electrons in the channel. (An electronvolt is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt.) Raising the gate’s voltage bends the band down at the surface and shortens the climb. That 0.06 is set by temperature alone, for anything that climbs a hill on heat.

And the gate can’t shorten the climb by its full voltage. Part of it is spent across the oxide, holding the charge left behind in the silicon as the gate pushes holes away; here, about five-sixths of each volt reaches the surface. So this transistor needs 72 millivolts on its gate for each tenfold, not 60. No ordinary gate can do better than 60, because it can’t move the hill further than its own voltage.

Picture 3

Tenfold per 72 millivolts

The channel’s electrons at every gate voltage, on a scale where each gridline up is a hundred times more, so a straight line means multiplying at a steady rate.

Below about 0.25 volts the curve is nearly straight, tenfold for every 72 to 80 millivolts: the switch going from off to on, a little less steep than the limit. Above about 0.4 volts the channel is formed, and fills more slowly, in proportion to the voltage past threshold. To switch a hundred-thousand-fold from 0 volts takes this transistor about 0.44 volts on its gate, and real chips add more on top, to switch fast.

So

Why heat sets the floor

Chips can run at lower voltage, and sometimes do, when there’s little work to do. But it costs. Squeeze the same switch into less voltage, and either it doesn’t turn fully off, leaking current and burning power even while it’s idle, or it doesn’t turn fully on, and switches slowly, so the same work takes longer while the leak goes on. Somewhere around half a volt, depending on the chip, the leak starts to eat the savings.

Both costs come from the same place: heat spreads the electrons’ energies, so each tenfold of current costs the gate at least 60 millivolts. Today’s transistors, their gates wrapped around a fin of silicon, come within about 10 millivolts of that limit.

Add it up: about 0.3 to 0.4 volts just to go from off to on, more on top to switch fast, and more again so that the worst of a chip’s billions of slightly different transistors still works. That’s roughly where 0.7 volts comes from.

And while we’re here

Other questions the pictures answer

Would a cold chip do better?

Sharper, yes. The 60 follows the thermal energy scale, kT, which falls with temperature: cooled with liquid nitrogen, to −196 °C, the limit is 15 millivolts per tenfold, and real transistors reach about 20 to 30. The controllers of some quantum computers run colder still, near 4 kelvin, to sit near their qubits, and switch more sharply as a bonus (there the swing stops following temperature, at about 10 to 20). For ordinary chips, so far, the cooling costs more than it saves.

Why is the oxide so thin?

The thinner the oxide, the stronger the gate’s grip on the channel: more of its voltage reaches the silicon, the switch gets nearer to 60, it carries more current when on, and the drain, close by in a tiny transistor, gets less say over the channel than the gate does. (That last is also why today’s gates wrap around a fin of silicon rather than lying on top.) But electrons tunnel straight through a thin oxide, and the gate leaks; at about 1.2 nanometres it could get no thinner. So since 2007 (Intel first, the rest by about 2011), chips have used thicker layers of hafnium-based materials, which hold more charge per volt than silicon’s oxide, with metal gates, so the gate keeps its grip without the leak.

Is the 60 millivolts a law?

For a switch whose gate can’t move the hill by more than its own voltage, yes. Researchers are trying ways round it: electrons tunnelling through the hill instead of climbing it, or gates whose materials amplify their own voltage, so the hill moves further than the gate. None is yet in mainstream chips.

Under the hood

How a transistor is simulated

The rules alone are textbook physics: the threshold voltage, and the subthreshold swing, (kT/q) ln 10 (1 + Cd/Cox). This page checks against them: its swing is 72 millivolts per tenfold, as the formula gives for this transistor (71 to 74), and its threshold is about a tenth of a volt from the textbook’s (the two define it differently).

What this simulation adds is the picture straight down: the bands bending, the climb, and where the channel’s electrons sit, from one rule for how electrons and holes diffuse and drift (the drift–diffusion model, the same rule that runs our LEDs, solar panels, lithium-ion battery and nerves).

It leaves things out, to run live. It’s a single slice straight down, at equilibrium, as a transistor is with no voltage between source and drain, and the current is taken as in proportion to the channel’s electrons. It’s a long transistor: in a short one the drain also pulls the hill down, worsening the swing and the leak, which is what drove gates onto fins. It’s classical: the surface field squeezes the channel’s electrons into quantum states, which shifts the threshold by 50 to 100 millivolts. It has no current tunnelling through the oxide and no traps at its face. And its 2 nanometres of oxide are a flat transistor’s of about 2000.
