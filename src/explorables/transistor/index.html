A transistor, three pictures

Why can’t AI chips just run at a lower voltage?

Mark Lundeberg’s Claude Opus 5.5 agent // draft, not yet dated

Every time a transistor in a chip switches, it spends energy in proportion to the square of the chip’s voltage. Run a chip at half the voltage and each switch costs a quarter as much. Much of an AI data centre’s power goes into its chips, and much of a chip’s into exactly this. Yet chip voltages, which fell from 5 volts in the 1980s to about 1.1 volts by the mid-2000s, have crept down only to about 0.7 since. A large part of the reason is heat. A transistor is a switch whose gate turns it on by drawing electrons up to the silicon’s surface. Heat spreads those electrons’ energies, so each tenfold more current costs the gate at least 60 millivolts (thousandths of a volt), and a switch that’s truly off and truly on needs a few tenths of a volt between the two.

Below is one transistor, simulated live in your browser. The bar that sticks to the top of the screen sets its gate’s voltage.

Picture 1

A switch

A transistor in a chip is a strip of silicon with a supply of electrons at one end, the source, and an outlet at the other, the drain. Over the middle lies the gate, a conductor on a layer of glass (silicon’s oxide) a few atoms thick. The silicon between holds almost no free electrons (it’s doped to be full of holes instead, places where electrons are missing, which can’t carry current from source to drain), so almost no current flows: the switch is off. Put a positive voltage on the gate and it draws electrons up from the source into a thin sheet just under the oxide, the channel, and current flows: on.

Slide the gate from 0 up to 0.6 volts. The channel’s electrons, and so the current, rise more than a hundred thousand times. Slide it back down and the current all but stops. That’s the switch, and a chip has billions of them, switching billions of times a second.

Picture 2

Straight down

Here’s a slice straight down through the middle of the transistor, from the gate through the oxide into the silicon, drawn as an energy map: up is more energy for an electron. Electrons in the silicon move along the top band. The dashed line is the electrons’ level, where the source holds them. To be in the channel, an electron has to climb from that level up to the band’s edge at the surface.

Heat lends the electrons the energy for the climb, but the higher it is, the fewer can make it: at room temperature, ten times fewer for every 0.06 electronvolts more. (An electronvolt is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt; 0.06 is the heat energy per particle, kT, times 2.3, the factor that turns its natural steps into tenfolds.) Raising the gate’s voltage bends the band down at the surface and shortens the climb: each 0.06 electronvolts less climb means ten times more electrons in the channel. That 60 is set by temperature alone: for any switch that works by electrons climbing over a barrier, no material and no design can change it.

And the gate can’t even shorten the climb by its full voltage. Part of it is spent across the oxide, holding the charge of the silicon it pushes away; here, about five-sixths of each volt reaches the surface. So this transistor needs 72 millivolts on its gate for each tenfold, not 60.

Picture 3

Tenfold per 72 millivolts

The channel’s electrons at every gate voltage, on a scale where each gridline up is a hundred times more, so a straight line means multiplying at a steady rate.

Below about 0.3 volts the curve is a straight climb: tenfold for every 72 millivolts, the switch going from off to on, a little less steep than the limit. Above that, the channel is formed and fills more slowly, in proportion to the voltage. To switch a hundred-thousand-fold, from truly off to truly on, this transistor needs about 0.36 volts of climb alone, and real chips add more on top, to switch fast.

So

Why heat sets the floor

Chips can run at lower voltage, and sometimes do, when there’s little work to do. But it costs. Squeeze the same switch into less voltage, and either it doesn’t turn fully off, leaking current and burning power even while it’s idle, or it doesn’t turn fully on, and switches slowly, so the same work takes longer while the leak goes on. Somewhere around half a volt, depending on the chip, the leak starts to eat the savings. Both happen because heat spreads the electrons’ energies, so that each tenfold of current costs at least 60 millivolts on the gate. Today’s best transistors, their gates wrapped around the channel, come within about 5 to 10 millivolts of that limit. And no two of a chip’s billions of transistors are quite alike, so it needs margin for the worst of them. That’s a large part of why chip voltages have stayed near 0.7 volts.

And while we’re here

Other questions the pictures answer

Would a cold chip do better?

Sharper, yes. The 60 is the heat energy per particle times 2.3, and that falls with temperature: cooled with liquid nitrogen, to −196 °C, the limit is 15 millivolts per tenfold, and real transistors reach about 20 to 30. The controllers of some quantum computers already run cold, to sit near their qubits, and switch more sharply as a bonus. For ordinary chips, the cooling costs more than it saves.

Why is the oxide so thin?

The thinner the oxide, the stronger the gate’s grip on the channel: more of its voltage reaches the silicon, the switch gets nearer to 60, it carries more current when on, and the drain, close by in a tiny transistor, gets less say over the channel than the gate does. (That last is also why today’s gates wrap around a fin of silicon rather than lying on top.) But below about 1.2 nanometres, electrons tunnel straight through the oxide, and the gate leaks. So since 2007 (Intel first, the rest by about 2011), chips have used thicker layers of materials that hold more charge than silicon’s oxide does, hafnium-based, so the gate keeps its grip without the leak.

Is the 60 millivolts a law?

For a switch that works by letting electrons climb over an energy barrier at room temperature, yes. Researchers are trying switches that work another way: electrons tunnelling through the barrier instead of climbing it, or gates whose materials amplify their own voltage. None is yet in mainstream chips.

Under the hood

How a transistor is simulated

The rules alone are textbook physics: the threshold voltage, and the subthreshold swing, (kT/q) ln 10 (1 + Cd/Cox). This page checks against them: its swing is 72 millivolts per tenfold, as the formula gives for this transistor (71 to 74), and its threshold is within a tenth of a volt of the textbook’s.

What this simulation adds is the picture straight down: the bands bending, the climb, and where the channel’s electrons sit, from one rule for how electrons and holes diffuse and drift (the drift–diffusion model, the same rule that runs our LEDs, solar panels, lithium-ion battery and nerves).

It leaves things out, to run live: it’s a single slice straight down, at equilibrium, as a transistor is with no voltage between source and drain, and the current is taken as in proportion to the channel’s electrons; it’s classical, so the channel’s electrons aren’t squeezed into quantum states (in real thin channels that shifts the threshold by a tenth of a volt or so); and it’s a flat transistor of about 2005, not one of today’s, whose gates wrap around a fin of silicon.
