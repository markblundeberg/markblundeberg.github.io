The Daniell cell, drawn four ways

One battery, four pictures

Zinc in zinc sulfate, copper in copper sulfate, a salt bridge between them. John Frederic Daniell's cell of 1836 is the first battery most chemistry students meet, and it makes about 1.10 V. Ask where those 1.10 volts actually are, and you get at least four different answers.

Each answer is a picture of the same cell. Each is correct, each makes something easy to see, and each hides something else. The bar just below this paragraph stays pinned to the top of the screen, and it drives every picture on this page at once: change what's in the beakers, connect a load, or swap the salt bridge for Daniell's original porous pot, and see what each picture does with it.

The crowd

Picture 1 · particles

Up close there is no voltage to see, only particles. At the zinc plate, atoms keep leaving the metal as Zn²⁺ ions, each leaving two electrons behind, and Zn²⁺ ions keep landing and turning back into metal. At the copper plate the same two-way traffic runs with Cu²⁺.

Set the load to open circuit (slider all the way left). Each plate's traffic now balances: as many ions leave as land. Equilibrium here doesn't mean nothing is happening. It means two opposite processes are tied.

Connect a load and the tie breaks. Zinc dissolves faster than it redeposits; the electrons it leaves behind run through the wire to the copper, where Cu²⁺ ions pick them up and plate out as metal. Inside the cell, ions close the circuit: positive ions drift toward the copper, negative ions toward the zinc, and in the bridge K⁺ and Cl⁻ carry the current across the gap.

Shows

That everything is motion, and that equilibrium is a balance of rates.

Hides

Why the answer is 1.10 volts and not 0.5 or 3. A voltage is an energy per charge, set by the statistics of all these particles together. No single particle carries it.

The ledger

Picture 2 · the textbook

A chemistry course turns the cell into bookkeeping. Each half-reaction gets a standard potential, measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE): −0.76 V for Zn²⁺/Zn, +0.34 V for Cu²⁺/Cu. The Nernst equation corrects each for its actual concentration, and the cell voltage is the difference.

Draw current and the ledger adds line items: the resistance of the electrolyte, an overpotential at each plate to drive its reaction, and a concentration term once the ions next to the plates start to run short (or pile up). It's quick, and it's usually right to within a few millivolts. That's why it's the textbook's picture.

Shows

The numbers, fast, and a clean list of where the voltage goes.

Hides

Position: the ledger never says where in the cell a loss happens. It also carries a quiet assumption, that both electrodes are measured against one and the same hydrogen reference. The "liquid junction" row is the price of that assumption, and textbooks usually set it to zero.

The levels

Picture 3 · electron levels, with the reference drawn in

Put position back, and plot what a voltmeter actually responds to: the energy of the electrons, expressed as a voltage. In each metal that's the Fermi level. For each redox couple in solution it's the redox level: the electron voltage at which the couple is balanced, with no net reaction either way.

Then do what the ledger doesn't, and draw the reference itself. The dash-dot line is where a standard hydrogen electrode would sit if you could dip one in at that exact spot. The standard potentials E° are distances measured up or down from that line (the dashed levels).

At open circuit each metal's Fermi level lies exactly on its couple's redox level, and the voltmeter reads the gap between the two metals. Connect a load and gaps open at both plates. Those are the overpotentials, and in this picture you read them straight off as voltage drops. The reference line isn't flat either. It tilts through the electrolyte as current flows, and it steps slightly at each liquid junction. The textbook's E(Cu) − E(Zn) equals the terminal voltage only when that line is level from one plate to the other.

Shows

Where each loss happens, as a voltage you can read, and where the reference really is.

Hides

Every ion that isn't in a redox couple. The sulfate, and the K⁺ and Cl⁻ that carry the current through the bridge, get no line at all.

Every species

Picture 4 · species voltages

The last picture gives every charged species its own line. Take each species' electrochemical potential (its free energy per particle, electrical part included) and divide by its charge. What comes out is a voltage, Vi = μ̃i / ziF, on the same axis as the electrons. Zinc ions, copper ions, sulfate, potassium, chloride and electrons all share one plot.

Read it like a landscape for current. Each species carries current down its own slope, and a flat line means that species carries none. Each ion also has a dashed companion, its standard level; the gap between the two encodes the ion's concentration on a log scale.

Switch to the full ladder, connect a load, and several things that were invisible become plain:

  • In the thin layer next to each plate, sulfate's line is flat. Sulfate isn't consumed at either plate, so in steady state it carries no current there. Zn²⁺ and Cu²⁺ bend instead.
  • In the bulk of each beaker, every line tilts together. That shared tilt is the ohmic drop, the same thing the ledger books as I·R.
  • In the bridge, K⁺ and Cl⁻ tilt: they're the ones carrying the current across.

Now compare with picture 3. In the chemists' convention used here (every element and H⁺(aq) counted at zero), the Zn²⁺ line is the zinc redox level, and the dashed V°(Zn²⁺) is its E° line. For zinc and copper, pictures 3 and 4 carry the same information. Picture 4 adds the ions that picture 3 leaves out, and drops the hydrogen reference, which it doesn't need.

Shows

Every ion's push, which ions carry the current where, and (next) the slow leak at the junctions.

Hides

Overpotentials read as one clean gap here only because each metal is its own reactant; in general they don't. The vertical offsets between different ions depend on conventions, so reading them takes practice.

What the Daniell cell is hiding

Textbooks describe an unloaded Daniell cell as being at equilibrium. It isn't, quite. Set the load to open circuit and look at the junctions in picture 4: wherever two solutions meet, lines that are flat on one side plunge or climb on the other. Zn²⁺ dives into the bridge; K⁺ dives into the zinc sulfate. A sloping line is a push, and a push means flow. Even with nothing connected, ions are diffusing out of their own compartment and into the next one; the bridge's K⁺ and Cl⁻ creep into both beakers, and a little zinc and copper sulfate creeps into the bridge.

That leak does two things. The first is a small liquid junction potential: the step in the hydrogen reference line in picture 3, and the "liquid junction" row in the ledger. A concentrated KCl bridge keeps it to a few millivolts, because K⁺ and Cl⁻ move at nearly the same speed and the junctions at the two ends of the bridge nearly cancel. Switch to the porous pot, Daniell's original design, and the two sulfate solutions meet directly. Make their concentrations very different and the junction potential grows past ten millivolts.

The second is slower and worse. Copper ions that wander all the way to the zinc plate are reduced right there, no wire needed, and the cell quietly discharges itself. Choose the porous pot, untick Hold steady in picture 1, and watch the zinc plate for copper-coloured flashes. Real Daniell cells were designed to slow this down, never to stop it.

Load makes all of it worse, because under current a junction can't hold still. At the zinc end of the bridge, Zn²⁺ keeps arriving from one side while K⁺ leaves from the other, and Cl⁻ is pushed out into the zinc sulfate. Real junctions drift and smear out. The diagrams here, like the textbook Henderson treatment behind them, hold each junction in place as a fixed mixing zone. That is a good approximation for the voltage, and a poor description of what the ions are actually doing. Hold steady is that approximation, made visible; untick it to watch the particles do what the diagrams won't.

So "equilibrium" is an approximation with a clock on it, good for minutes to hours but not forever. The particles show the leak happening; picture 4 shows it as slopes. The ledger books it as a correction assumed to be zero, and picture 3 shows only its side effect, the step in the reference.

About this page

Written and built by Claude (Opus 5.5, an AI model made by Anthropic), using the species-voltage framework from Mark Lundeberg's web book Electrochemical species band diagrams, which works through these ideas in much more depth. Mark set the brief; the words, the code and any mistakes are mine.

Every picture is driven by one small model of the cell, solved live in your browser. It's a quasi-steady snapshot: it treats the beakers and bridge as large enough that their contents don't change while you watch. No real cell under load is in steady state. The beakers slowly change composition as it discharges, and the salt bridge slowly empties into them even at rest; the pictures show one moment of that slow drift.

  • Ideal dilute solutions, with activity equal to concentration in mol/L. Real sulfate solutions are strongly non-ideal (activity coefficients near 0.15 at 0.1 M), which shifts real voltages by tens of millivolts without changing any of the pictures.
  • Standard levels from standard Gibbs energies of formation at 25 °C, in the chemists' convention (elements and H⁺(aq) at zero): V°i = ΔG°f,i / ziF.
  • Liquid junctions as fixed linear mixing zones with Henderson-style transport, using diffusion coefficients at infinite dilution. Under current a real junction drifts and broadens; the model holds it fixed.
  • A stirred bulk, with 50 µm diffusion layers at each plate, and symmetric Butler–Volmer kinetics with illustrative exchange currents of 0.2 mA/cm² (zinc) and 0.1 mA/cm² (copper).
  • 1 cm² plates with 5 mm of electrolyte in front of each; a 2 cm long, 1 cm² bridge of 1 M KCl (real bridges often use saturated KCl, about 3.5 M), or a 3 mm porous wall with 30 % open area.
  • Position axes are schematic: the 50 µm diffusion layers and the 2 cm bridge are drawn at comparable widths.
  • The particle picture is schematic too: counts scale with the logarithm of concentration, and the beakers are topped up to behave like a large, stirred cell.
