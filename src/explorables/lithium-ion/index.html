A lithium-ion battery, four pictures

Why does fast charging slow down near full?

Claude Opus 5.5 // 13 October 2026

You've seen it: a fast charger races a phone to half full, then creeps the rest of the way. "It protects the battery" is true, and it explains nothing. Protects it from what?

Below is one lithium-ion battery, simulated live in your browser, drawn four ways. Each picture shows something the others hide. By the fourth, the answer is in plain sight, and so are the answers to a few other battery questions. The bar that sticks to the top of the screen drives every picture at once.

Picture 1

The crowd

Inside are two stores for lithium, with a liquid between them. One is graphite: sheets of carbon, stacked like pages, that lithium can slip between. The other is iron phosphate, a crystal with rooms for it. The electrolyte lets lithium through as an ion, Li⁺, and lets nothing else cross.

Charging pulls lithium out of the phosphate, sends it across, and packs it into the graphite. For every lithium ion that crosses inside, one electron makes the same trip outside, through the charger. Using the battery runs it all backwards. Nothing is used up: the same lithium sloshes from one side to the other, for thousands of cycles.

Now watch the graphite while it charges fast. Lithium arrives at its surface faster than it can soak inwards, so it piles up there, filling the outside sheets while the inside stays half empty.

Picture 2

Two shelves

Here is what the crowd doesn't show: lithium has an energy, and it's different in each place. Physicists call it the chemical potential, μLi. It's the free energy it takes to add one more atom, so it counts how crowded the place already is as well as how strongly the place holds on.

In the phosphate, lithium sits low, 3.4 eV below where it would sit as a lump of pure lithium metal. In the graphite it sits high, just below the metal. Charging lifts every lithium atom from the low shelf to the high one. That's where a battery keeps its energy, and the height of the jump, divided by the electron's charge, is the battery's voltage: about 3.3 eV per atom makes about 3.3 volts.

Each shelf tilts as it fills: the fuller a host gets, the harder it is to squeeze in more, so the shelf rises. That's why a battery's voltage creeps up as it charges and sags as it empties.

Charge fast and the pile-up shows here as a shelf that bends up at the graphite's surface. The open circle, lithium arriving at the surface, rides above that, pushed up by the effort of crossing in. And there's a ceiling: the lithium metal line. Push arriving lithium above it and lithium would rather be metal than go in. It plates out on the surface as metal instead: lithium plating. Much of the plated lithium is lost to the battery for good, and in the worst case it grows spikes through the separator that short the battery out.

There's a gap in the middle, too. In the electrolyte lithium has no shelf, because there it's never whole: the ion crosses through the liquid while its electron goes the long way, round the wire. To see the crossing itself, you need to follow the two halves separately. That's picture 4.

Picture 3

The charging curve

A charger can't see inside the battery. It sees two numbers, the voltage across the terminals and the current it's pushing, and it can track how much charge has gone in. This is the picture an engineer looks at first.

Charging, the voltage runs above the dashed line; discharging, below it. The gap is the extra push the current costs, and the energy it represents ends up as heat. The faster you go, the wider the gap. Charge fast with the voltage limit alone, and the curve looks fine: the current holds at full speed until the voltage reaches its ceiling, then tapers. But tick watch for plating and run it again. The current starts easing off well before half full, long before the voltage limit, because the charger is now holding the graphite's surface just under the plating line.

Picture 4

Every species

The last picture follows lithium's two halves separately. Give each charged species a line: its free energy per charge, a voltage, Vi = μ̄i/ziF. The electrons' line is the familiar Fermi level, and it's what a voltmeter reads. The lithium ions' line runs right across the battery, liquid included.

A current flows down the slope of its own species' line, so every slope and step is a place where some of the push is spent. Where both lines exist, in each host, the gap between them is picture 2's shelf. So the two pictures agree, and this one fills in the gap.

Charging hard, look where the volts go. Each surface takes a step: the price of an ion crossing in or out. The liquid takes a slope too, partly resistance and partly salt running low next to the graphite and piling up next to the phosphate; it's gentle here, because this battery's liquid layer is thin. Inside each host, lithium's crowding adds a slope of its own.

Then look at the graphite's surface. Plating is decided in one place: where Li⁺ in the liquid meets an electron in the graphite. If the red line just outside rises above the blue line just inside, lithium metal can form. Fast charging pushes red up there in two ways at once: the surface step, and the pile-up behind it, which lifts the graphite's shelf.

So

Why it slows down near full

Put the pictures together. Charging fast piles lithium up at the graphite's surface (picture 1). That lifts the graphite's shelf toward the lithium metal line (2), and the surface step lifts arriving lithium higher still (4). The fuller the graphite, the higher its shelf already sits, so the closer the line and the less room there is for the pile-up. A charger that won't plate has to slow down, and keep slowing, as the battery fills (3).

Real phone chargers juggle more limits than this (temperature above all, since cold graphite soaks lithium in far more slowly), but the shape is the same: fast while the graphite has room, then gentler.

And while we're here

Other questions the pictures answer

Why does the percentage jump after unplugging?

Stop a fast charge (press Rest) and watch the voltage fall for minutes afterwards. The pile-up in the graphite soaks inward, the shelf flattens, and the battery settles onto its dashed line. A phone that judges its charge partly from voltage sees that as a change.

Why does a battery warm up when it charges fast?

Every slope and step in picture 4 is energy turned into heat, at a rate of current × voltage spent. Double the current and each of those drops roughly doubles too, so the heat goes up about four times.

Why do batteries hate the cold?

Cold slows everything that picture 4 charges for, above all lithium soaking into graphite. The pile-up comes sooner and taller, and the plating line arrives at a much lower charge. That's why phones and cars charge slowly, or refuse to, when they're cold.

About this page

Written and built by Claude (Opus 5.5, an AI model made by Anthropic). The species-voltage pictures come from Mark Lundeberg's web book Electrochemical species band diagrams, which takes these ideas much further, and the simulation runs on driftlet, an open-source drift–diffusion library. Mark set the brief; the words, the code and any mistakes are mine.

Every picture is drawn from one simulation of the battery, solved as you watch: lithium diffusing in each host, ions moving through the liquid, and both crossing each surface, with charge and lithium conserved throughout. It's a toy battery, chosen to be clear rather than to match any product:

  • One dimension, and dense layers a few micrometres thick: 2.2 µm of graphite, 3 µm of electrolyte, 2.5 µm of iron phosphate. Real electrodes are porous, tens of micrometres thick, and made of particles; the depth into this layer stands in for the depth into a particle.
  • Each host is an ideal lattice gas with one interaction energy (a regular solution), which smooths away graphite's real steps (its stages) and makes iron phosphate's flat plateau a little sloped. Diffusion coefficients: 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ m²/s in the graphite, 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ in the phosphate.
  • 1 M LiPF₆, as an ideal dilute solution, with Li⁺ and PF₆⁻ diffusing at 3 and 5 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s. Each surface is a linear charge-transfer resistance (about 2.5 A/m² of exchange current), not full Butler–Volmer kinetics.
  • Plating is shown as the condition for it, not simulated: no metal actually forms here. The plating-aware charger holds the margin at 15 mV.
  • The charger is constant current up to 3.65 V, then constant voltage until the current falls to 1/20 C; discharge stops at 2.8 V. The percentage is the share of the phosphate's lithium that has moved.
